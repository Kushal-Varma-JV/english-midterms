📝 Revision Notes

1. The narrator's frame

The author says the story came to him "in a flash", from a chance meeting with an old man in a coconut grove near Chennarayapatna. He keeps it short because elaborating would make it less authentic.

2. The old man and the owner

  • The old man arrived after walking hundreds of miles and stayed on. He knew agriculture and understood the workers' problems.
  • Petty thefts stopped, income rose dramatically, and the 10-acre farm grew "beyond imagination".
  • The owner became lethargic and gained wealth, prestige and many friends. His wife feared adultery and other vices that came with his new clout. Their life was "getting out of hand".
  • She couldn't decide whether the old man's arrival was for the better or for the worse.

3. The story within the story

  • At sunset by the well, he tells her about Tammanna, whose most important "possession" was his rival.
  • He slips on the rival's name (Sangoji, then Basavaiah) and remarks how facts take new forms when told as fiction.
  • She feels it is "none of my concern", but stays so as not to hurt him.

4. Stages of the rivalry (visible → invisible)

  1. Land & friends: 4 acres for 4 acres, 10 friends vs 15 admirers. At first it looked like healthy competition.
  2. No land left: 1,000 vs 800 acres. Tammanna refuses to sell 200 acres, so Basavaiah seizes them by force and fences them.
  3. Art: supporters suggest court, police or attack, but Tammanna wants to annihilate him completely. He composes ballads about his life, including Basavaiah's cruelty and meanness.
  4. Basavaiah's failed replies: he tries to sing and fails, then works harder on the farm, which doesn't help either. He keeps encroaching on Tammanna's land, but Tammanna, lost in art, doesn't even notice.
  5. Fame: folklore scholars and critics pursue Tammanna, and he is honoured as the best poet of his times.
  6. Material wealth: Basavaiah builds a palatial mansion, pays people to praise him and wears gold and diamonds. His house still feels empty without Tammanna's books, so he invites scholars, poets and musicians.
  7. Health: Tammanna falls ill, and Basavaiah's spirits revive. "Tammanna's disease was Basavaiah's health."
  8. Death: Tammanna separates his songs from his body. He gives up everything and leaves as if dead. A few days later Basavaiah dies, with no more reason to live.
  9. Forgetting: Tammanna forgets all his songs and becomes a non-entity. "Thus I avenged myself."

5. The revelation

  • The idea came when Russia told America "I am not your enemy". America felt agony and boredom without its enemy. A nation can survive that, but a person cannot.
  • After a certain age, man loses his name and is just "an old man". Then he reveals: "My name is Tammanna."
  • He warns that her husband is not amenable to advice, because man lives for revenge and challenge till death.
  • He asks her to forget it or treat it as a dream, then goes to visit Lokya's sick child.
  • The narrator ends: "I had seen all this in a dream."

Quick answers

  • Qualities that impressed the narrator: strong build, native intelligence, eyes full of memories, and being labourer, overseer and philosopher in one.
  • Significance of "hundreds of miles": he had left his old life behind completely, a man "running away" after faking his own "death".
  • Why the wife worried: sudden wealth made her husband lazy, and she feared vices and adultery.
  • When he told his story: when he sensed her plight (her husband's life going out of hand).
  • Basavaiah's ray of hope: Tammanna's illness.
  • Why Basavaiah invited scholars: to fill his empty house with meaning, since he had no art of his own.

🎯 Themes & Analysis

1. Rivalry as the reason to live

Man needs wealth, education and art, yet lives for "unbearable vengefulness". The rival is Tammanna's "most important possession". When one rival is gone, the other loses his purpose, as Basavaiah dies within days.

2. Competition vs rivalry

This answers the pre-reading question. It starts as healthy competition (land, friends) and becomes a destructive rivalry (force, humiliation, wishing illness on the other).

3. Visible → invisible

The contest moves from material things (land, friends, mansion, jewels) to abstract things (art, fame, health, death and finally forgetting). The final, ironic "weapon" is to become nobody.

4. Wealth vs art

Basavaiah's riches feel empty without Tammanna's books. Money cannot buy meaning. It can only borrow it by inviting scholars and poets.

5. Strange human nature & revenge

The story reads like a parable. Tammanna's revenge is not to kill his rival but to take himself away. The owner now repeats Tammanna's early life (10 acres, rising wealth, many friends), which shows the cycle goes on.

6. Russia–America parallel

This widens the story from two villagers to two nations, an allusion to the end of the Cold War. Nations, like people, can depend on having an enemy. The difference is that a nation survives losing its enemy, while a person may not.

7. Identity & age

"After a particular age, man loses his name." The once-famous poet is now just "the old man", "your servant". Losing his identity is both his punishment and his revenge.

Narrative technique: stories within stories

  • Layer 1: the narrator/author ("seen in a dream")
  • Layer 2: the old man and the owner's wife (the frame)
  • Layer 3: Tammanna vs Basavaiah (the tale)
  • Layer 4: Russia vs America (the global parallel)

Mixing fact and fiction (the name slip, "not a real story", "a dream") hides the truth at first and then reveals it. The layers show that human relationships are too complicated for one simple telling.

Tone & style: reflective, mysterious and philosophical. It is brief and parable-like, and ends on an ambiguous, dream-like note.

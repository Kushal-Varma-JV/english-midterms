To the Foot from its Child

Pablo Neruda
Poem (translated from Spanish by Alastair Reid)
⚡ Tip Sheet 📝 Revision Notes 🎯 Themes & Analysis

⚡ Quick Tip Sheet

👤 Author

  • Pablo Neruda: 1904–1973
  • Chilean poet, diplomat and politician
  • Real name: Neftali Ricardo Reyes Basoalto
  • "Pablo Neruda" was his pen name, and later his legal name
  • Pen name taken from the Czech poet Jan Neruda
  • Nobel Prize for Literature: 1971
  • Recognised as a poet when he was a teenager
  • Styles: surrealist poems, historical epics, political manifestos, prose autobiography, love poems
  • Love poems: Twenty Love Poems and a Song of Despair (1924)

🌐 Translator

  • Alastair Reid: born 1926
  • One of Scotland's foremost literary figures
  • Craftsman in poetry, prose and translation
  • Left Scotland during World War II, then lived in Spain, France, Switzerland, the USA and South America
  • Published 40+ books
  • 'Weathering' (1978): early selected poems & translations
  • 'Oases' (1997): prose & poetry about his friendship with Graves, Neruda and Borges

📝 Revision Notes

1. The dreaming child's foot

A baby's foot does not yet know it is "just a foot". It dreams of being a butterfly (freedom, flight) or an apple (beauty, fulfilment). This shows the free, unlimited imagination of childhood.

2. Reality teaches its lesson

  • Over time, stones, glass, streets, ladders and rough paths teach the foot that it cannot fly or become a fruit.
  • The foot "loses the battle" and becomes a prisoner condemned to live in a shoe. Reality has defeated its dreams.

3. Life in the dark shoe

Inside the shoe it learns about the world in its own way. It is cut off from its fellow foot and gropes through life like a blind man. This stands for the loneliness and narrowness of adult life.

4. Physical change

  • Soft nails like quartz turn opaque and hard as horn.
  • Petal-like toes become bunched and shapeless, like eyeless reptiles or worms.
  • Calluses and corns appear: the "faint volcanoes of death". The foot finds this coarsening hard to accept.

5. Endless toil

The "blind thing" walks without rest, hour after hour, as a man's or a woman's foot, through fields, mines, markets and ministries. It is barely freed from the shoe, even for love or sleep, and walks on until the whole man chooses to stop (death).

6. Death & the return of the dream

The foot goes underground, into total darkness, unaware. It never knew it had stopped being a foot. Perhaps it is being buried so that it can finally fly or become an apple, just as it once dreamed.

Quick answers

  • What would the foot like to be? A butterfly or an apple.
  • What does time teach? That it cannot fly or become a fruit, so dreams must give way to reality.
  • Why defeated? Life's hardships crush its dreams and it is locked in a shoe.
  • Words for real experiences: stones, bits of glass, streets, ladders, rough earth, shoe, dark, callused, volcanoes of death, toils.
  • Lines showing transformation: "These soft nails of quartz… grow hard… hard as horn", "petaled toes… eyeless reptiles… like worms", "Later, they grow callused".
  • "Until the whole man chooses to stop": until the person dies. The foot's work ends only with life.
  • Title: the foot is treated as a person with a childhood. The poem is like a message to the grown, weary foot from the dreaming "child" it once was.
  • Beginning vs end: the foot is unaware at birth ("not yet aware it's a foot") and unaware at death ("never knew it had ceased to be a foot"). Life comes full circle, from innocence back to unknowing.
  • Parallels in nature: a buried seed grows into a tree bearing apples. A caterpillar in its dark cocoon becomes a butterfly. The body returns to the soil and feeds new life.

🎯 Themes & Analysis

1. Colourful dreams vs humdrum reality

The butterfly and apple (freedom, beauty) are set against stones, shoes, darkness and toil. Growing up means slowly giving up childhood dreams.

2. Society crushes dreams into rigid moulds

The shoe stands for the limits society puts on a person: rules, duties and work. The foot is "condemned", like a prisoner, with no choice. Neruda gently criticises how people are forced to fit a fixed shape.

3. Salute to the ordinary human

Though defeated, the foot keeps walking "without respite" through fields, mines, markets and ministries. It stands for every worker, man or woman, who carries on bravely against all odds. The poem honours this quiet endurance.

4. The cycle of life

Birth, growth, struggle, ageing and death. The foot's changes (soft to hard, petals to worms) trace the ageing of the human body.

5. Hope beyond death

The ending is open. Burial may be a new beginning, where the foot at last "flies" or "becomes an apple". This links death to rebirth in nature.

6. "Foot" as the key image

  • The foot stands for the whole person, the lowliest and hardest-working part of the body.
  • The shoe stands for prison and social constraint.
  • The butterfly and apple stand for dreams, freedom and fulfilment.
  • Darkness stands for ignorance, isolation and finally death.

By using an everyday body part, Neruda makes a grand theme (the human life journey) feel simple and physical.

Tone & style: reflective and melancholic, with a hint of hope at the end. It is written in free verse with short, broken lines, and uses vivid, almost surreal images.

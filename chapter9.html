I Believe That Books Will Never Disappear

Interview with Jorge Luis Borges

  • Born in Buenos Aires

📝 Revision Notes

1. First reading & education

Borges' first literary reading was Grimm's Fairy Tales in English. He says his father's library educated him perhaps more than school or university did.

2. His mother, Dona Leonor

  • He remembers her kindness above all. She was intelligent, gracious and had no enemies.
  • He feels guilty: he wasn't a happy man, so he couldn't give her the happiness she deserved. He should have been more understanding.
  • He makes it a general truth: all children take their mother for granted, like the moon, the sun or the seasons, and only realise it after she dies.

3. Blindness as a resource

  • Blindness is now a way of life, "not entirely unhappy".
  • A writer (and every person) should treat whatever happens as a resource. Humiliations, misfortunes and embarrassments are raw material, like clay, for shaping art.
  • Homer said the same in the Odyssey: the gods send ruin so that there may be songs for future generations.
  • One of Borges' poems says misfortunes are given so we may transmute them into eternal works.

4. "All that is near becomes far" (Goethe)

Goethe meant the sunset, and also life: all things leave us. For Borges, the visible world has moved away forever, but it has been replaced by other things. His duty is to accept and enjoy them. He still pretends he isn't blind, buys books and fills his house with them.

5. The dream & the history of the book

He dreamed of a great library burning, perhaps the Library of Alexandria. Alifano suggests he owes readers a history of the book. Borges likes the idea but doubts an 83-year-old can take it on, and notes that Spengler (Decline of the West) wrote remarkable comments on books before him.

6. The book goes beyond its author

  • He agrees with Shaw: a book worth re-reading is "written by the spirit".
  • The author's intention is a meager, fallible thing. Every book has something more, which is mysterious.
  • Reading an ancient book is like reading all the time that has passed since it was written.
  • A book may have errors and we may disagree with it, but it still keeps something sacred, magical, and happiness-giving.

7. Poetry

  • Poetry is too intimate and essential to define without oversimplifying, like the colour yellow, love or autumn leaves.
  • Poetry is not the poem, which is only symbols. It is the aesthetic act that happens when the poet writes and when the reader reads, a little differently each time.
  • It is magical, mysterious and unexplainable, but not incomprehensible. If the reader feels nothing, the poet has failed.

8. Precise words

Precise words bring out the emotion. In Dickinson's "This quiet dust was gentlemen and Ladies", the idea (we all become the dust of death) is a banal cliché, but "gentlemen and ladies" gives it magic. "Men and women" would have been trivial and failed as poetry.

9. Metaphors

True metaphors have existed from the beginning of time. Only the way we express them changes. The essential ones are time/river, life/dreams, death/sleep, stars/eyes and flowers/women. The others are whimsical. The poet's task is to discover metaphors.

10. Why books will never disappear

  • Some say modern communication will replace books with something faster. Borges says that is impossible.
  • Other inventions extend our body (voice, sight, arms). Only the book extends imagination and memory.
  • Literature is "a controlled dream". We owe it almost everything we were, are and will be.
  • The past is a sequence of dreams, and books are the memory of all centuries. If books disappear, history and man would disappear.

Quick answers

  • Why does he pretend he isn't blind? To accept his blindness positively and keep enjoying life. He still buys books and fills his house with them.
  • How does a book go beyond the author's intention? It holds something mysterious and more than the author meant. Each age and reader finds new meaning in it.
  • When does the poetic act happen? When the poet writes it and when the reader reads it.
  • How should we view humiliations & misfortunes? As resources and raw material (clay) to shape into art.
  • Can his remorse be generalised? Yes. He says it is true of all children, who take their mothers for granted until they die.

🎯 Themes & Analysis

1. The permanence of books

This is the title's central claim. Books are unique because they extend the mind (imagination and memory), not the body. They are humanity's memory, so losing them would mean losing history and humanity itself.

2. Turning suffering into art

Blindness, humiliation and misfortune are "clay" for art. Homer and Borges' own poem support this. It is a positive, accepting attitude to life's hardships.

3. Loss & acceptance

"All that is near becomes far." Everything leaves us: sight, loved ones, life itself. Borges doesn't complain. He accepts the loss and enjoys what replaces it.

4. Guilt & taking loved ones for granted

His honest confession about his mother turns a personal regret into a universal truth about children and parents.

5. The reader's role

Meaning isn't fixed by the author. Poetry happens in the reading, and a book grows richer over time. The reader is as important as the writer.

6. The magic of language

The Dickinson example shows that precise word choice turns a cliché into poetry. Metaphors are ancient and universal, and poets rediscover them.

7. Literature, dreams & memory

Literature is a "controlled dream". Dreaming and remembering the past are almost the same thing. This links to Borges' favourite themes: dreams, libraries, infinity. His dream of the burning Library of Alexandria shows his fear of losing books.

Form: the interview

It is a question-and-answer format. Alifano's short, respectful questions lead Borges into long, reflective answers. It moves from personal topics (childhood, mother, blindness) to universal ideas (books, poetry, metaphor, literature).

Tone & style: reflective, philosophical, humble and optimistic. The answers are rich in literary references (Homer, Goethe, Shaw, Spengler, Dickinson).
